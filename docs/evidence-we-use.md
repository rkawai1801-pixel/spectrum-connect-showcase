# Which research we use, and why

This page is written for parents and for reviewers. It explains which kinds of research the engine
counts as evidence, which kinds it leaves out, and why. Ray asked for this page on 7 October 2026,
with one worry in mind: the thought **"if it worked for this one child, it would work for mine."**

## Why we leave out case reports

A **case report** is a paper about one child, or a few children. A doctor writes down what happened
after a treatment. Case reports can be useful to doctors, because they can point to something new
worth studying. But a case report cannot tell a parent whether a treatment will help their own
child. It cannot even show that the treatment caused the change in that one child.
(Nissen and Wynn, *BMC Research Notes*, 2014, 7:264,
[doi.org/10.1186/1756-0500-7-264](https://doi.org/10.1186/1756-0500-7-264).)

Here is why one story can mislead:

| Reason | What it means |
|---|---|
| **No one to compare with** | We only see the child who got the treatment, not what would have happened to that same child without it. |
| **Children grow and change on their own** | A change that came with time can look like a change that came from the treatment. |
| **Hard times often get better on their own** | Families often try something new when things are at their worst. A very hard stretch is often followed by a better one anyway. Scientists call this "regression to the mean". (Barnett and others, *International Journal of Epidemiology*, 2005, [doi.org/10.1093/ije/dyh299](https://doi.org/10.1093/ije/dyh299).) |
| **Many things change at once** | A new school year, a new therapist, better sleep and a new diet can all happen in the same months. |
| **The good stories get told more** | A story where a treatment seemed to work is more likely to be written up and shared than one where nothing happened. |
| **Every child is different** | Even if a treatment really helped one child, that child may differ from yours. |

## What a fair test looks like

In a **randomised trial**, many children are put into groups by chance, like a coin toss. One group
gets the treatment and one does not. Because the groups are made by chance, growing up, good and bad
stretches, and life changes happen in both groups. So a difference between the groups is more likely
to come from the treatment.

Even one trial can be a fluke. That is why the engine looks first for **systematic reviews**, where
researchers find every trial on a question using written steps, and judge how well each one was done.

## The order we look

1. **Systematic reviews first**, with or without a meta-analysis (which adds the numbers from many
   trials together). Each review's quality is checked with AMSTAR 2.
2. **If no good review exists, the studies themselves.** The engine reads randomised trials, and also
   studies that compare a treated group with an untreated group. Each study's quality is checked with
   RoB 2 or ROBINS-I. The report then says it is the engine's own summary of the studies.

A weak review is **not** thrown away. It stays in, and its low rating travels with it.

## Other papers we leave out or set aside

Every paper in PubMed has a label that says what kind of paper it is. Before any AI reads a paper, a
simple rule reads that label and sorts the paper. A person later checks the study type of every
paper that is kept.

| Kind of paper | Why it can sound convincing | What we do, and why |
|---|---|---|
| **Expert summaries with no written search** ("narrative reviews") | Written by an expert, with many references | **Background only.** The writer does not say how they found their studies, so they could cite the ones that agree with them. We never take a number or a finding from one. |
| **Scoping reviews** | Has "review" in the name, lists many studies | **Left out.** It maps what has been studied but does not judge whether the studies were done well. |
| **Preprints** | Looks like a finished paper | **Left out.** No other experts have checked it yet. The published version can be considered later. |
| **Trial plans** ("protocols") | Names the treatment and why it might work | **Left out.** A plan has no results yet. The results paper can be considered later. |
| **Editorials, comments and letters** | Printed in a medical journal | **Left out.** An opinion, not a test. If it points to a study, we look at that study. |
| **Retracted papers** | Was once published | **Left out.** A paper the journal has taken back cannot support any finding. Retractions are checked when a paper comes in, and again later. |
| **Guidelines and expert consensus statements** | Comes from a trusted group | **Background only** (Ray's rule, October 2026). They are summaries of evidence, not new evidence. |
| **Case-control and cross-sectional studies**, on treatment questions | Can show a link between two things | **Left out of treatment questions, with the reason shown.** They can show that two things go together, but not that a treatment caused a change. |

## Not every study counts the same

The engine uses **GRADE** to say how sure we can be about an answer: High, Moderate, Low or Very Low.
Randomised trials start at High and move down if there are problems, such as too few children.
Studies that only watch what happens start at Low. (Balshem and others, *Journal of Clinical
Epidemiology*, 2011, [doi.org/10.1016/j.jclinepi.2010.07.015](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jclinepi.2010.07.015).)

So those studies are not thrown away. They count, and the report says how sure the answer is.

## What "not enough research" means

There are two different "not supported" labels, and the difference matters:

- **"Not supported: not enough research yet."** The fair tests are missing or too weak. This does
  **not** mean the treatment was shown not to work.
- **"Not supported: studied, no benefit found."** Good studies looked, and found no real benefit.

A story about one child does not change either label, because a case report is not part of the
evidence we count.
